01

The season

A season is a world tour. Every stop is a round, and every round hands out points.

Formula 1 runs from March to December. Each round is one Grand Prix weekend at one circuit, and the teams pack up and fly to the next one, often a week later.

This season has its rounds spread across the world.

Points from every race add up to two championships running at the same time. The drivers' championship crowns the best driver. The constructors' championship crowns the best team, and a team's score is simply both of its drivers' points added together.

02

The race weekend

Friday is for learning, Saturday decides the order, Sunday pays the points.

Most weekends follow the same rhythm: practice to find the right set-up, qualifying to set the starting order, then the race. A few weekends a year are sprint weekends, which swap two practice sessions for a short extra race on Saturday.

Quick check

Which session sets the starting grid for Sunday's race?

Qualifying sets the race grid. On sprint weekends, sprint qualifying sets the grid for the sprint only.

03

Qualifying

One fast lap at a time, in three rounds. The slowest are knocked out after each.

Qualifying lasts about an hour and comes in three parts. In Q1 (18 minutes) every car sets lap times and the slowest are knocked out. Q2 (15 minutes) knocks out the next group. The last ten fight it out in Q3 (12 minutes). The fastest lap of all takes pole position: first place on the grid.

04

Lights out

Five red lights, a pause nobody can predict, then about 300 km of racing.

Cars line up in qualifying order, two by two. After a formation lap to warm the tyres, five red lights come on one by one, a second apart. After a short random pause they all go out together, and the race is on. Move before they go out and it's a jump start, which earns a penalty.

Try it. Drivers react in about two tenths of a second.

A Grand Prix covers just over 305 km, usually 50 to 70 laps depending on the track, and can't run longer than two hours. In the dry, every car has to stop at least once to change tyres (chapter 5 explains why). The first car across the line when the chequered flag waves wins.

Quick check

When does the race start?

It's "lights out and away we go": the start is the moment all five lights go out.

05

Tyres

Softer tyres are faster but wear out sooner. Every race is a trade between grip and life.

All teams use the same supplier, Pirelli. For each race Pirelli brings three dry compounds, always marked the same way: red soft, yellow medium and white hard. When the track is wet, drivers switch to grooved tyres that clear water: green intermediates for a damp track and blue full wets for heavy rain.

As a tyre wears, it loses grip and each lap gets a little slower. That is called degradation. A soft starts fastest but fades quickly; a hard starts slower but keeps going. Eventually any tyre hits a cliff, where grip falls away fast. That's the moment to stop for new ones.

One rule shapes every dry race: each driver must use at least two different dry compounds. That means at least one pit stop, so tyre choice and timing become the race's main tactic.

06

Pit stops and strategy

A stop costs about 20 seconds. Fresh tyres have to win that time back.

Changing four tyres takes a crew about two to three seconds. The real cost is the trip through the pit lane, where there's a speed limit: in total a stop costs around 20 to 25 seconds compared with staying out. Strategy is deciding when to pay that price, and how many times.

Below, three cars with the same pace race 56 laps on different plans. Change the tyre wear and your pit lap, then press play.

Two moves come up in every race. The undercut: pit a lap or two before the car ahead, then use fresh tyres to lap faster while they're still out on worn ones, and come out in front once they stop. The overcut is the reverse: stay out longer and hope your old tyres hold on while the other car warms up its new ones.

Quick check

Pitting before a rival to jump ahead of them on fresher tyres is called…

The undercut: stop first, go faster on new tyres, come out ahead when the rival stops.

07

Safety car and flags

When there's danger, the race is slowed down. That can undo a whole afternoon's work.

After a crash or debris, marshals need the cars to slow down. There are two ways to do it. The safety car drives out in front of the leader and everyone queues up behind it, with no overtaking, so the gaps between cars disappear. The virtual safety car (VSC) has no car at all: every driver must slow to the same reduced pace, so the gaps stay roughly where they were.

Switch between the three and watch what happens to the gaps.

That's why a safety car can change a race. A leader's 20-second cushion is gone in a few laps. Pitting also becomes cheap: with the field crawling, a stop costs roughly half the usual time, so teams often call their drivers in the moment it comes out.

08

Points and the title

The top ten score in every race. The title is won when nobody can catch up any more.

A win is worth 25 points, and the top ten all score. Sprints pay the top eight, from 8 points down to 1. There's no bonus for the fastest lap any more; that was dropped in 2025.

The championship is decided the moment the leader is further ahead than the points still left to win. Nobody needs to win the last race; the maths simply runs out for everyone else.

Quick check

One team's drivers finish 3rd and 5th. How many constructors' points does the team score?

3rd pays 15 and 5th pays 10. The team scores both: 25.

09

Reading timing data

Every lap is split into three sectors, and colours tell you who is quickest where.

Timing screens split each lap into three sectors. Colours do the talking: purple means the fastest of anyone so far, green is a driver's own best, and yellow is slower than their best.

Add a driver's three best sectors from different laps and you get their ideal lap: what they could do if everything came together at once. The gap between the ideal and the real best shows how much time was left out there.

Quick check

A purple sector on the timing screen means…

Purple is the overall best. Green is a personal best; yellow is slower.
